## Angle summaries

### [Curve systems from type and curve design](01-curve-systems.md)

Type-design and curve-design tools have tried for 45 years to describe shapes by relationships instead of fixed vectors, and they agree on one architecture. A skeleton ("spine") curve comes first. Points are declared relative to other points or curves (midway, intersection, at a fraction along). A pen or width profile then expands the skeleton into outlines, and the result is converted to cubic Béziers at a set error tolerance for output. On which curves read as "natural" or "perfect", the research is clear. The eye notices where curvature peaks and how it changes. The best primitives keep curvature monotone between designer-placed points and continuous across them (G2): the Euler spiral/clothoid, and its generalisation, the log-aesthetic curve family. That family includes the logarithmic spiral, and the golden spiral is just a log spiral whose radius of curvature grows at a fixed rate with arc length. So "make the flame's leaf-tip curve golden" becomes an exact, checkable property, not a quarter-circle approximation. Clustered parallel lines can be harder than they look. Offsets of Béziers are not Béziers, and they form cusps wherever the offset distance reaches the radius of curvature, so families of lines should be produced by solving or interpolating from analytic spines. On usability, METAFONT, Prototypo and Nupuram all show that writing shapes as programs and exposing many sliders loses designers. What works is few on-curve points with a small set of point types (Spiro), named parametric parts with bounded sliders (Glyphs smart components, Prototypo's "advised" ranges), live previews with construction overlays, and letting the designer start from a drawing that the engine fits to a primitive. For this engine: analytic dominant curves anchored to the page box, positions on them by arc length, motifs attached with G2 continuity, width/hatch envelopes, Bézier fitting for SVG, and validators (curvature comb, golden-pitch readout, offset-vs-radius check) so the golden-ratio rules are measured, not eyeballed.

### [Computational ornament generation](02-ornament-generation.md)

The ornament-generation literature keeps arriving at the same architecture as the user's "dominant functions" idea. First a skeleton of controlling curves is laid down: Jones's parent stem, Day's "vertebrate" scroll spine, Wong et al.'s rhythmic controlling lines (which they call analogy), FLOWPAK's direction guides. Every secondary element is then attached in the local frame of a parent curve at a parameter t, meets it tangentially (Jones, Prop. 12), and is scaled and rotated by a rule instead of being placed freehand. Every system that regenerates for a new size or shape separates a cheap layout proxy (Wong's proxies, FLOWPAK's spine plus left/right width profile, the skeletal-stroke backbone) from the expensive final rendering. That separation is what makes "adapt to any page size" workable. Curves that read as natural have continuously varying curvature (Ruskin and Dresser; Xu and Mould's magnetic curves). The logarithmic spiral is the special case curvature = 1/(b·s), and the golden spiral is b = 2·ln(φ)/π ≈ 0.306. So if one leaf is copied by repeatedly applying a single spiral similarity about the scroll's pole, the leaf tips land on that golden spiral by construction, with no after-the-fact fitting (this is my derivation, not a source claim). Balance and even spacing are enforced by measuring empty space directly: largest empty circle on a medial-axis transform, evenly spaced streamlines with d_gap/d_stop, and iterative grow/shift refinement. Work also runs in hierarchical phases: frame, then scrolls, then leaves, then fillers. Interlace is a solved combinatorial problem: put a crossing at each edge midpoint of a graph (Mercat), alternate over/under along each strand, and choose crossing counts as the page resizes so the number of loops stays as designed (for a rectangular plait, loops = gcd of the edge bounce counts). The main warnings: purely local greedy growth looks jumbled (Wong's own failed willow pattern), physics-style curve generators are hard to steer, the good optimizers are slow (FLOWPAK takes about an hour for 50 elements) and must run offline, and I found no turnkey JS/TS ornament engine. The nearest architectural precedent is Adobe's Deco, whose models are JavaScript "scriptals" and which shipped inside Flash and Photoshop.

### [Golden ratio and natural growth geometry](03-golden-growth.md)

The defensible core of "golden ratio in ornament" is self-similarity, not the number 1.618. D'Arcy Thompson's gnomon theorem says that when a structure is built from parts that are similar and similarly placed, corresponding points (tips, bases, curl eyes) lie on logarithmic spirals. So the engine should generate leaves and curls by repeating one spiral-similarity transform (scale times rotation about a pole). "Golden" then just means fixing the scale step at phi per 90 degrees (b = ln(phi)/(pi/2) = 0.30635, pitch 17.03 degrees, 6.854x growth per turn). Sharp's table shows other phi-derived log spirals exist: phi per 54, 108 or 144 degrees, from pentagon and golden-triangle gnomons. That lets the designer pick how tight a curl is and still stay phi-derived. The empirical and historical record argues for honesty. Markowsky shows phi claims only hold within roughly 2% and that people cannot tell 1.6 from 1.7 rectangles. Sharp measured the nautilus at about 3x per turn, not 6.85x. The Renaissance Ionic volutes (Serlio, Salviati, Goldmann) are compass-built, piecewise-Archimedean arc chains, not golden spirals. Hübner (2024) found that people prefer a true golden spiral to the quarter-circle Fibonacci spiral mainly because its curvature is continuous. So what makes ornament read as "printed" is exact repetition under one transform plus curvature continuity (Euler-spiral easements, log-aesthetic curves), not phi to five decimals. For the designer, the workable pattern has three parts. First, a few named parameters (pole, growth rule, angular step, entry tangent and curvature). Second, on-curve authoring in the style of Spiro rather than Bezier handles. Third, a fit, report and snap loop that takes their Illustrator tracing, measures how close it is to golden and corrects it inside a stated tolerance. A conformal "design flat, wrap into spiral" mapping (z = exp(w), as in de Smit and Lenstra's analysis of Escher) is a strong way to let one master leaf be drawn once and stamped through a scroll.

### [Digital engraving and security printing](04-engraving-print.md)

Professional engraved and security-printed line work does not look "printed" because of texture. It looks printed because every line belongs to a family generated from a few guide curves, and line weight is a continuous function, not a fixed stroke width. Ostromoukhov's Digital Facial Engraving shows the core model. A designer draws four border curves in Illustrator, the engine re-parametrizes them by arc length, builds a Coons-patch grid, and turns iso-parametric lines into engraved lines by thresholding a sawtooth "universal copperplate". Lines therefore swell and taper to nothing on their own, and layers merge by min/max rules to make cross-hatching. DigiDürer gives a second model that maps closely to "dominant functions": each line is a propagated front (equipotential) of an initial curve, with spacing set by a speed field. It also names the three properties that make classical line work read well: lines never cross, run roughly parallel and stay smooth. Real rose-engine guilloché supplies the answer for "logical spread". One waveform is repeated N times, and only a fixed step and a deterministic phase sequence change between cuts: constant, half-pitch for barleycorn, a linear ramp, a zigzag, or even Fibonacci for the "sinewave" moiré. Guilloche software makes the same idea explicit with envelopes: a sinusoid is mapped between an inner and an outer envelope curve. Commercial security tools (Agfa Arziro as an Illustrator plug-in, Jura Corvina Engraver) confirm the workflow is hybrid. Designers draw guide paths and freehand key strokes, and generators fill in the line families. In the browser, the realistic split is crisp vector geometry, with variable width baked into filled outline polygons because SVG has no variable-width stroke. A separate, static "ink" layer can add ink gain (blur plus threshold, or dilate) and very low-amplitude displacement. WebGL threshold shading is the option for very dense fills. Animated or large-area feTurbulence/feDisplacementMap is a known performance and crash risk, and noise quickly reads as grunge rather than engraving.

### [Runtime-generated graphics on the web](05-web-runtime-systems.md)

The web systems that generate decoration at runtime mostly share one architecture: a pure, deterministic function of (box size, named parameters, seed) produces geometry, the geometry is cached against exactly the inputs that change it, and a thin renderer turns it into SVG or canvas output. A ResizeObserver plus a size-diff check decides when to re-run that function. This is what the CSS Paint API spec requires of worklets, and it is how Wired Elements, Rough Notation and Excalidraw are built. Nobody has shipped the owner's specific requirement, a dominant curve that every other element hangs from. The closest working precedents are: Rive's Follow Path constraint, which attaches a part at a fraction of arc length and turns it to match the tangent; Tyler Hobbs' flow fields, where one field steers every line and a minimum spacing gives the "logical spread"; and shan-shui-inf's plan-then-draw pipeline, where a planner lays out the skeleton and the renderer then draws strokes along it. The "printed, not hand-drawn" quality comes from three things: building each line as a filled outline with a mathematical width profile (perfect-freehand, shan-shui's stroke(), Bezier.js graduated outlines) rather than a CSS stroke-width; computing parallel line groups as exact offsets; and keeping every random choice behind a fixed seed (or removing randomness entirely), so redrawing at a new size never reshuffles the art. Golden-ratio enforcement already exists on the web for typography (Modular Scale, Utopia): one ratio, numbered steps, and interpolation between two designed end sizes. The same token approach can drive leaf sizes, spiral growth and band spacing. The realistic designer workflow combines what these systems do: the designer draws skeleton curves and leaf archetypes in Illustrator, the engine imports them (Paper.js importSVG) and derives everything else, tunable values are declared in a parameter manifest that becomes sliders (as houdini.how's JSON manifests and Stripe.dev's "design instrument" did), and the designer generates several candidates, picks one and pins its seed (as the MIT Media Lab identity did). The CSS Paint API teaches the right contract but should not be the delivery mechanism. It outputs raster, Firefox has no support, Safari ships it disabled by default, and its polyfill and showcase site have been abandoned.

### [Designer tooling for parametric design](06-designer-tooling.md)

Across the tools that worked for designers who are not full-time programmers, the common move is that the designer keeps drawing and the tool turns the drawing into structure. Grasshopper references a hand-drawn Rhino curve as a live driver. Illustrator pattern brushes and Glyphs corner, segment and cap components hold tiles in named slots. Glyphs smart components interpolate between "extreme" drawings that share the same point structure. In Cuttle, Apparatus, Sketch-n-Sketch and Bret Victor's prototype, canvas gestures edit a program. For this engine that points to five features. (1) Illustrator stays the surface for drawing motifs (leaf, flame tongue, crest parts) and rough "dominant" curves, which go on named construction layers. (2) The engine fits those curves to exact families (golden or log spiral, clothoid, kappa-curve), reports how far the drawing deviates, and only then snaps the curve to phi. It never infers this silently. (3) Every ornament is placed in arc-length and normal coordinates along a spine, using Inkscape's Pattern Along Path formula, with whole-number tile counts and Illustrator-style "stretch between guides" so leaf tips are never distorted. (4) A browser workbench offers a short, named parameter panel with JSON presets, on-canvas handles bound to chosen parameters with phi-locked constants frozen, editors that draw the spacing law for clustered lines, and a contact sheet showing the frame at many page sizes. (5) Recursion stops when parts get too small to render, not at a fixed depth, so fine engraved detail holds at every size. The pitfalls are well documented: unstructured "spaghetti" graphs, and parametric models that must be rebuilt when their topology changes (Davis found 97.5% of 2,002 Grasshopper models had no modules). Apparatus's drag solver jumps between solutions, Sketch-n-Sketch generates code nobody can read, Spiro curves blow up when points move too far, and round-tripping SVG through Illustrator is unreliable.

## Drawing analysis: implications

- The drawings are not phi-governed now. Of all the measured ratios, only the flame tip curve comes near golden, and even there the best b is 0.49, not 0.306. The exact relations that do exist are Illustrator operations: similarity copies (flame leaves, crest hearts at 1/sqrt(2), ring loops), mirrors (crest, side-leaf, the diagonal in the medallion), exact repeats (scallop pitch 9.00) and snapping ends onto a circle. An engine should formalise these four operations (copy-along, scale-about, mirror, attach-to-curve) first and add phi as a parameter choice on top. The designer already works this way, which makes it realistic to design in.
- Flame: rebuild it as one log-spiral 'spine' through the tips, with N leaves placed at equal angular steps. The leaf prototype gets scale proportional to spine radius and rotation equal to the spiral tangent angle plus a constant. With b=0.306 instead of the drawn 0.49, each tip moves about 1.6u RMS (max 2.4u) on a 119x209 piece, roughly 1-2% of the width and 3-5 stroke widths. That is visible on close comparison but keeps the same character; a free-pole golden fit moves the tips only 0.39 RMS (max 0.69u). The bigger visual change is the leaf sizes. They currently shrink linearly (1 -> 0.27); a self-similar rule would make them shrink geometrically, about 0.875 per 25-deg step at golden b, or 0.82 at b=0.49, so the smallest leaves would get noticeably larger.
- Leaf shapes: each leaf side is one cubic with a mid-segment curvature peak, so no side is a log spiral. To get leaves that are 'perfectly golden', the engine has to generate each side as a log-spiral or clothoid arc (or a G2 spline with a prescribed curvature profile) and then fit cubics for SVG output. Snapping the existing control points will not do it. Measured local b of 0.21 (back) and 0.38/0.59 (front S) sit on either side of 0.306, so a golden leaf template is a plausible stylisation.
- Medallion sweep: the S-curves are clothoids (curvature about linear in arc length, R2 0.98), and the ring part is a circle of radius about 1.2x the medallion radius. The natural 'dominant function' is therefore a circle-plus-Euler-spiral spline (as in Raph Levien's Spiro/Euler-spiral splines), not a golden spiral. Forcing a golden log spiral onto the limbs costs 1.3 RMS and 3.2u max with the wrong look. Phi can still set radii, for example ring radius = phi^k x medallion radius (it is 1.20 now; phi^0.5=1.27 would grow the ring about 5u), and the stem spacing (middle/outer gap 1.467 -> phi adds 1.7u).
- Curls: the right scroll (#103) is a tight volute (b 0.14-0.16, about 2.5x growth per turn), and its inner part fits Archimedean equally well. A golden curl (6.85x per turn) is clearly more open; at the same outer size it shows about half as many turns. Expose 'turns' and 'b' as separate parameters, and let the designer choose golden b or a volute.
- Nested line fans: the spacing is near-even with 10-40% jitter, and lines converge exactly on anchor points on the medallion circle. An engine can generate them as offsets or interpolations between two guide curves (Illustrator-blend-like), with a spacing law that is either uniform or geometric with ratio r. The drawn data supports uniform spacing (or r of about 1.1-1.15 at the open end), not r=phi; phi spacing (1.618 per step) would collapse the inner lines and look very different. The uniform/geometric choice should be a dial, not a hard-coded phi rule.
- Because 62% of medallion stroke ends sit on other strokes (and fans sit on ellipse extremes and specific angles), the engine's core data model should be an attachment graph: each element is parameterised by the curve it hangs from (parameter t or angle on its parent) plus a local transform. Re-flowing to any page size then means moving the dominant curves, and the dependents follow.
- Design workflow: the designer can keep drawing in Illustrator, with the engine fitting parameters back out, which is what these scripts do. Spiral pole and b, copy scale and rotation, mirror axis and attachment are all recoverable automatically, with residuals reported (copies at 0.003u, ring at 0.5u RMS). A 'fit then snap with a tolerance report' loop (for example 'snapping to golden moves tips 0.4-2.4u') is realistic. Note the look-elsewhere caveat: any ratio is within 12% of some half-power of phi, so the engine should impose phi by construction and not hunt for it in drawings.
